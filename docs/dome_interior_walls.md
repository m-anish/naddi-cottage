# Dome interior walls — options

## Summary

Line the walls only and leave the roof as it is. Its 80 mm PUF panels are already insulated and quiet, and the off-white inner face looks right. On the walls, fix **8 mm fibre-cement board everywhere**, limewash it above the 3'-3" sill line, and fix **vertical pine boards over it below** (Scheme 2), with the roof struts painted to match the roof. Lining the \~495 sq ft of wall costs about **₹0.8–1.5 lakh**. Casing the ten roof struts in pine adds ₹18,000–42,000, and it can be done any time later.

With a wood floor, the wainscot isn't too much as long as its tone differs from the floor's: natural pine over a darker floor, limewashed or painted boards over a pine one. See [the with-and-without renders](#m2n0rf31870.18559).

Behind the lining, keep the planks able to dry:

- **Foam:** run the 2" thermocol continuously over the inner face of the vertical purlins. Your 1×2" purlins then leave a 2" open, drained gap behind the planks, and the foam finishes flush with the 2×4" struts.
- **Battens:** fix horizontal battens across the purlins, screwed through the foam. The board fixes to these on every wall; below the sill line, the pine fixes over the board on timber strips screwed into the same battens.
- **Steel:** a thin strip of foam tape on the face of each 2×4" strut, where a batten crosses it, is enough. The foam already covers the purlins, and timber battens are a fair thermal break themselves.

Do the walls first, the wood floor second and the skirting last.

## Get the build-up right first

Fix what sits behind the finish before choosing the finish. Your planks get damp on the inside in heavy rain and then dry because the inside is open. Pack 2" thermocol against them and close it in, and that water is trapped against foam and steel: mould, rust and a musty smell within a few monsoons.

From the outside in:

1. **Fibre-cement planks**, existing.
2. **A 50 mm drainage gap** behind them, which is simply the depth of your 1×2" purlins. At the foot, close it off from the floor void with insect mesh and a small flashing that slopes out over the tin skirting, so drips weep outside instead of into the sealed void. Leave the top open under the eaves, behind mesh, so air dries it.
3. **A breather membrane** (vapour-open housewrap such as Tyvek or a local equivalent) on the inner face of the purlins, lapped top over bottom. It sheds drips but lets vapour out. If you can't source one, keep the gap anyway; the rule is that foam never touches the planks.
4. **50 mm thermocol** as one continuous layer over the inner face of the purlins, on the membrane. With 1×2" purlins inside 2×4" struts, it finishes flush with the struts' inner faces; cut it neatly around them. Use the flame-retardant construction grade, not packaging foam, and foam-seal the joints. EPS is combustible and must be fully covered, and it soaks up more water than XPS ([Studio Matrx](https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/eps-insulation-india)), another reason to keep it off the wet planks.
5. **Foam tape on the struts only.** Over the purlins the thermocol already sits between steel and batten. Where a batten crosses a 2×4" strut, put a 3–6 mm strip of closed-cell foam tape on the strut face; that is all the steel needs. Avoid metal battens: they would carry the winter cold straight to the lining, where it shows as damp stripes.
6. **25 mm treated timber battens**, run horizontally at 16" centres across the vertical purlins and screwed through the foam into each one. Carpenters may call them runners or furring strips; the pine boards and cement board fix to these. The void behind the lining carries wiring and switch boxes, so nobody cuts into the foam.
7. **8 mm fibre-cement board on every wall, full height**, so the foam is sealed behind non-combustible board everywhere, even behind the pine. 8 mm is enough on battens at 16" centres; use 10 mm on any lower wall left without pine, and 12 mm near a stove. Lay the boards long side horizontal so their long edges land on the battens (16" spacing divides a 4 ft board exactly), with a short timber block behind each vertical joint. Fix with fibre-cement countersunk screws at 300 mm, at least 15 mm from edges. Tape and fill joints with a fibre-cement compound and alkali-resistant mesh, not gypsum compound, and use flexible sealant or a timber cover strip where facets meet at a fold.

   **The wainscot (Scheme 2)**: 15 mm timber strips screwed through the board into the battens at the same 16" spacing, then vertical pine boards secret-nailed to the strips. Oil the backs of the boards first and leave a small gap at the floor and under the ledge. The pine stands about 25–30 mm proud of the upper wall and the datum ledge covers the step; at the two doors, make the frames deep enough for the pine to stop against them.

Skip polythene behind the lining in the main room. On sunny days after rain, moisture moves inward from the damp planks, and polythene would trap it at the lining. Sealed foam joints and a painted lining slow vapour enough. The bathroom keeps its vapour layer because it makes steam every day.

**Finish on the board.** Limewash needs an absorbent surface, and fibre-cement board is dense and smooth. Prime it with a mineral primer or a thin lime skim (about 1.5–2 mm) over the whole surface, then brush on 2–4 thin coats, above about 8 °C ([Kreidezeit](https://www.kreidezeit.de/wp-content/uploads/TDS_limewash_2307_EN.pdf), [Studio Matrx](https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/lime-wash-walls-india)). It chalks slightly and can't be scrubbed, which matters little above the wainscot. A matte emulsion over an alkali-resistant primer is the low-upkeep alternative; avoid oil or enamel paint.

**Why not marine ply instead of board?** Ply holds screws anywhere, cuts easily and won't crack at the folds, and 9 mm costs only about ₹10,000–15,000 more over the upper walls ([Greenply](https://www.greenply.com/blogs/rate-of-marine-plywood-in-india-waterproof-sheet-prices-and-standard-sizes-explained)). But it burns and would leave the foam unprotected, and limewash won't take on wood. Keep board, and add timber strips behind it where shelves will go. If you do use ply, IS 303 BWP is enough indoors; marine IS 710 is made for boats ([Ply Reporter](https://plyreporter.com/article/153905/PL)).

If you plan a bukhari, line the wall behind it and around the flue with cement board, never timber over foam. Rats like warm foam cavities, so mesh every opening of the gap and seal the foot of the lining.

### Order of work

Board the walls before the wood floor goes down. The pine, ledge and skirting are finishes and go on after it. That keeps cutting dust, limewash drips and dropped tools off the finished floor.

1. Mark the finished floor level on the walls first: base board + underlay + wood. Measure the 3'-3" datum and switch heights from that line.
2. Make the dome weather-tight. Then fit the membrane, thermocol and battens, and the wiring in conduit with its boxes.
3. Fix the 8 mm board on every wall, stopping 10–15 mm above the base board. Tape and fill the joints above the 3'-3" line only, then limewash or paint the upper walls, taking the finish a little below the line.
4. Lay the wood floor once the wet, dusty work is done and the room has dried out. Leave its expansion gap at the walls.
5. Fix the timber strips and pine boards, then the datum ledge and skirting, which cover the gaps. Then apply the final coat.

Your underfloor is already well built: the void is sealed in painted tin on the sides and underneath, lined with 1" foam, over HDPE and weed-control fabric (also sold as landscape fabric or geotextile). Two things remain. Keep the wall gap draining outside, not into that void, as the drawing shows. And lay a thin insulating underlay, such as cork or foam, under the wood: 1" of foam is modest for a Naddi winter, and the underlay is a cheap top-up that also quietens footfall.

### Wiring

Run the wiring before the board goes up, and put every switch and socket in the wainscot band:

- **Depth:** with board, strips and pine, there is about 57 mm from the pine face back to the foam, enough for standard boxes. The upper walls stay clean, with no plates in the shallow zone above the datum. Switches just under the ledge, at about 3 ft, are easy to reach.
- **Conduit:** run every cable in conduit, never loose against the thermocol. PVC cable insulation in contact with polystyrene loses its plasticiser and turns brittle ([WorkSafe Queensland](https://www.worksafe.qld.gov.au/news-and-events/newsletters/esafe-newsletters/esafe-editions/esafe-electrical/2021-bulletins/august-2021/tpspvc-cables-in-contact-with-polystyrene-products)). Conduit also protects the cables from rats and screws. FRLS wire is a good choice.
- **Boxes:** metal boxes screwed to a timber block, flush with the pine face. Keep every joint inside a box you can open.
- **Earth and RCCB:** bond the steel frame to earth and fit a 30 mA RCCB. In a steel dome, a nicked cable can make the whole frame live; earthed, it trips the breaker instead.
- **Layout:** sockets at least 6–8" above the floor, not under windows that open, and none on the wall behind a bukhari. Centre plates on a board or a board joint. Run cables only straight up or across from each box, and photograph every wall with a tape measure in shot before boarding.
- **Between walls:** the sealed underfloor void is a good route, in conduit, with every entry sealed.

## Wall schemes under the white roof

Every view stands near the main door looking toward the washroom door, under the assumed loft. The roof is the same off-white PUF panel in all of them; only the walls and the treatment of the ten roof struts change. On the walls, the natural place to change material is the 3'-3" sill line.

### 1. Lime walls

Cement board limewashed warm white on every wall, under the off-white roof with painted struts. It is the brightest and cheapest scheme, and the whole dome reads as one pale shell. The walls by the daybed and doors will scuff, and the room has no warmth at hand height.

&#91;image: Scheme 1: lime walls\]

### 2. Timber wainscot, roof struts painted (recommended)

Vertical pine boards fixed over the board to the sill line, finished with a timber ledge, and limewash above. The roof struts are painted the roof's off-white, so the roof reads as one calm tent and the timber stays at hand height. The pine takes knocks from furniture and shoulders, and the ledge doubles as a deep sill and shelf.

&#91;image: Scheme 2: timber wainscot, roof struts painted\]

### Is 3'-3" of wood too much with a wood floor?

Not if the boards and the floor are different tones. What looks heavy is the floor and the lower walls in the same wood: the bottom third of the room turns one colour (F, further down). Over a floor that is clearly darker or lighter than the boards, the wainscot reads as its own band, and the room stays light because two-thirds of every wall and the whole roof are off-white.

&#91;image: With and without the 3'-3" wainscot: A no wainscot, B natural pine, C limewashed pine, D painted boards\]

So choose the floor first, then finish the boards to suit it:

- **Floor darker than pine** (walnut, teak or a stained board): natural pine (B).
- **Floor in pine or a similar tone:** limewashed pine (C), with a white-pigmented oil or a thinned white stain wiped on so the grain still shows; or painted boards (D) in sage, clay or slate. You keep the knock-proof boards and the ledge without the merging.
- **Brightest room or tightest budget:** no wainscot (A). Keep a 4–6" timber skirting and the datum ledge, and expect scuffs by the daybed.

Don't lower the band to use less wood. The 3'-3" line matches the window sills, and a lower band would cut across them; changing the tone does more than changing the height. While the floor is undecided, limewashed pine (C) is the safest choice: it suits any floor tone and keeps the most light of the three wainscots. Natural pine (B) is the warmest if the floor ends up clearly darker.

Here is the case to avoid. With a honey-pine floor and natural pine boards (F), floor and lower walls merge into one wood colour, and with the pine ladder and loft as well, the room starts to feel like a box. The same floor with no wainscot (E), or with limewashed or painted boards, keeps the floor as the one warm surface.

&#91;image: If the floor is the same tone as the pine: E no wainscot, F natural pine wainscot\]

### 3. Timber wainscot, roof struts cased in pine

The same walls, with the ten roof struts boxed in pine. The struts draw the dome's star pattern overhead and tie the timber below to the loft and ladder. It costs ₹18,000–42,000 more, and because it only adds to Scheme 2, you can do it next year.

&#91;image: Scheme 3: wainscot with pine-cased roof struts\]

### 4. Expressed frame

Limewashed walls with every strut, on the walls and the roof, cased in pine, so the dome's geometry becomes the decoration. It is the most "geodesic" scheme but the most carpentry: every wall panel is cut to fit between members. (This render predates your roof note, so its roof is limewash rather than PUF panel; the look is nearly the same.)

&#91;image: Scheme 4: expressed frame\]

### 5. Pine walls

Pine boards on every wall under the white roof: the Himachali cabin, lightened by the pale ceiling. It is warm and forgiving of knocks, but darker at eye level, and it puts the most combustible material over the foam. If you choose it, use cement board near any stove and oil the pine rather than varnish it so it yellows less.

&#91;image: Scheme 5: pine walls\]

## Lining materials compared

Cement board and pine boards are the two to build with. Cement board is cheap, damp-proof and non-combustible; pine is warm, light and easy to cut into triangles. Prices are 2026 Indian ranges from the linked sources; life is my estimate unless a source gives it.

| Gypsum board, taped and painted | 110–190; 130–210 moisture-resistant ([Studio Matrx](https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/false-ceiling-cost-guide-india)) | 20+ years if kept dry | Fails if wet | Good | Hard, reflective | Joints crack at the folds when the steel frame moves; needs flexible sealant or shadow gaps |
| Pine tongue-and-groove, 12 mm | Board ₹19–121 ([IndiaMART](https://m.indiamart.com/impcat/pine-panel.html)); \~130–280 with battens, labour and oil (my build-up) | 25+ years with re-oiling | Fine if ventilated; moves with humidity | Combustible | Warmer, slightly absorptive | Light and easy to cut to triangles. | Plywood with natural veneer | BWP board ₹160–350 ([Greenply](https://www.greenply.com/blogs/plywood-price-in-india-2026-per-sq-ft-cost-ply-rates-and-how-to-choose-the-right-one)) + veneer and polish | 20+ years | BWP grade is fine | Combustible | Reflective | One or two sheets per facet; modern and crisp, but costly |

| PVC panels | \~70–400 ([DesignCafe](https://www.designcafe.com/blog/home-interiors/wall-panelling-cost-guide/)) | 7–12 years | Waterproof | Smoky if it burns | Reflective | Not recommended for the main room |
| Wood-wool acoustic board | Board ₹32–90 ([IndiaMART](https://m.indiamart.com/impcat/wood-wool-boards.html)) | Long | Tolerant | Low combustibility | Absorbs sound well | Only if the room still rings once furnished |

On sound, rugs, curtains, the daybed and a loft bed will absorb most of the echo in a dome this size. Owners report furnished small domes sound much like ordinary houses ([Monolithic Dome forum](https://forum.monolithicdome.com/t/sound-echos-in-domes/114)). One correction: a wood floor reflects sound rather than absorbing it, so the rugs do that work. If the room still rings once furnished, hang a fabric or wood-wool panel high on one wall.

## Indicative cost of each scheme

The wall schemes cost ₹0.6–1.9 lakh, and their ranges overlap, so choose on looks and upkeep. Each figure covers the \~495 sq ft of wall lining (battens, boards, finish, labour) plus the roof struts. My estimated rates, built from the table above:

- ₹110–210/sq ft for cement board with limewash
- ₹130–280/sq ft for pine boards
- ₹150–350 per running foot for pine strut casings
- ₹200–400 per foot for the datum ledge
- ₹25–50 per foot to paint the 120 ft of roof struts

Common to all schemes, on top: 50 mm EPS for the walls at ₹30–90/sq ft ([Studio Matrx](https://www.studiomatrx.org/guides/eps-insulation-india)), about ₹15,000–45,000; foam tape at the strut crossings, a few thousand; and the breather membrane, to be quoted locally.
