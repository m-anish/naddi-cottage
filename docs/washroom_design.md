# Dome-extension washroom — design proposal

Oct 26, 2026 · @Anish

## Summary

Build **Layout A**: a dry zone at the front with the WC on the left slope and the vanity on the right, and the shower at the back under the ridge, beside the existing small window. A raked 8 mm glass screen with a 2'-0" centre door separates the two zones.

Three moves make this awkward tent-shaped room work:

- **A 2'-9" vertical service ledge** along both slopes. It squares off the low corners you cannot stand in, hides every pipe and the WC cistern, and doubles as shelf and vanity top.
- **A vertical door "portal".** The front wall leans in over the floor by about 1'-9" at door height, so a door set in that plane would make you duck. A small box with a 6'-8" soffit keeps the door upright.
- **Standing tasks on the centre line.** Only about 20 sq ft of the 66.5 sq ft floor has 6'-0" or more of headroom. Entry, basin and shower use that strip; the WC and vanity tuck against the slopes, where you sit or bend.

For finishes I recommend **Palette 1, Slate & Lime**: local Himachal slate floor, lime-white walls, micro-cement in the shower, deodar and brass. **Palette 2, Bright & Panelised** uses porcelain, WPC and compact laminate panels; it installs dry and fast. **Palette 3, Kota & Whitewash** is the best value. It keeps the same waterproof build with Kota stone, 6×6" ceramic, whitewash and chrome, and uses a tiled partition with a curtain instead of glass. Indicative all-in costs are **₹2.5–5.3 lakh**, **₹2.2–4.8 lakh** and **₹1.4–3.0 lakh** respectively. In every palette, build the floor as an insulated concrete slab on stone fill.

## How to build it

Build bottom-up and test the waterproofing before any finish goes on. Naddi sits above McLeod Ganj (about 1,830 m); Dharamsala averages about 3,000 mm of rain a year and upper areas see winter snow, so damp, condensation and cold pipes are the main risks.

1. **Close the perimeter.** Build a stone or brick plinth wall under the back and side floor beams, down to firm ground, so the fill is contained and rain and rodents stay out. Anti-termite treatment and pipe sleeves go in now.
2. **Lay the drainage before filling.** 110 mm soil pipe from the WC; 75 mm from the dry-zone floor trap and the shower's linear drain; steady fall to the septic or sewer. Water-test every joint before burying.
3. **Fill.** Compacted rubble in 150 mm layers, sand blinding, then a 200 µm polythene damp-proof membrane lapped up the plinth.
4. **Insulate under the slab.** 50 mm XPS laid on the membrane, with a 25 mm XPS strip standing up around the slab edge. You get a foam-insulated floor without a hollow void under it.
5. **Slab.** 75–100 mm PCC (M15–M20) with mesh, laid to falls: about 1:80 to the floor trap in the dry zone, 1:50–1:60 to the shower drain. Keep the threshold level with the main room. Then build the two ledges (and Palette 3's partition) in 4½" brick on the slab, leaving chases for pipes and a recess for the cistern.
6. **Walls.**
   1. Wire-brush the iron purlins and coat them with zinc-rich primer; they will be sealed in for good.
   2. Fit 50 mm insulation between the purlins behind the existing fibre-cement skin.
   3. Add a vapour-retarding layer on the warm (room) side, then GI battens.
   4. Fix 12 mm cement board on the slopes and back wall wherever tiles or micro-cement will go; the brick ledges are plastered instead. Dry-zone linings follow the chosen palette.
7. **Waterproof.** Two-coat polymer cementitious membrane: the whole floor with a 300 mm upturn in the dry zone, and every shower surface full height, including slopes, back wall and ledge tops. Tape all corners and pipe penetrations. Pond-test for 48 hours.
8. **Finish.** Floor, ledge tops, wall finishes, then glass last. On the overhanging slopes use small, light tiles or panels with a C2-class adhesive; large heavy tiles on a surface leaning over you are a falling risk.
9. **Ventilation.** A 150 mm exhaust fan high on the right slope near the ridge, ducted outside with a back-draft shutter and rain hood. Steam collects at the 10'-0" front apex, so the fan goes high, not near the window. Keep the small window openable and undercut the door 10–15 mm for make-up air.
10. **Electrical.** Splash-rated fittings in the shower zone, RCD protection, and a dedicated circuit for the geyser.
11. **Hot water.** A 15 L storage geyser fits high on the inside of the front wall, above the door portal and out of the spray; a 25 L unit is tight there and may need to sit on the main-room side of the partition. If you want lower running costs, add a solar evacuated-tube heater outside with the geyser as backup; HIMURJA has run subsidies for these, so check the current scheme. Lag every exposed pipe against winter frost.

### Floor: concrete slab or a cement-board deck?

Use stone and cement for the floor. Tiles and stone can go on cement board, but a raised board floor brings risks this wet, cold room doesn't need. You still get the foam insulation by laying it under the slab, as in step 4.

|  | Stone fill + insulated concrete slab | Raised cement-board deck on steel joists |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tile or stone on top | Yes; the best base for both | Yes, if stiff enough: joists at 16" (400 mm) or closer, sagging no more than span/360 for tile or span/720 for stone, or grout and stone crack |
| Shower falls | Formed in the concrete | Joists packed to a slope, or a mortar screed added on top |
| A leak | A damp patch you can see | Soaks the board edges and rusts hidden joists for years before it shows |
| WC fixing | Bolts into concrete | Needs extra steel members under the WC |
| Brick ledges and partition | Sit on the slab | Too heavy; they must be framed instead |
| Winter | Insulated slab stays close to room temperature | The void holds outdoor air, and pipes in it can freeze. Foam laid on the ground leaves the floor cold; it has to be fixed under the boards |
| Sealing the void with tin | No void to seal | Traps ground moisture, which condenses on cold steel. Tin rusts at the ground line in \~3,000 mm of rain a year, and any gap lets rats in |
| Underfoot | Solid | Can sound hollow under stone |

For a floor this size the two cost roughly the same, so the choice is about risk. Close the outside with a stone plinth wall pointed in cement rather than tin. It lasts as long as the slab and gives the ledges a solid base.

A raised deck only makes sense if the ground is more than about 3 ft below floor level or slopes steeply. In that case: steel joists at 16" centres, two layers of board, a sheet waterproofing membrane, XPS fixed between the joists, polythene on the ground, and a ventilated, mesh-screened skirting.

**Side walls.** Cement board is the right base on the slopes in every palette, because masonry cannot follow a 65° wall that leans over you. Tiles and thin stone both work on it if they stay light. Use ceramic, or calibrated slate about 10 mm thick, in small sizes, set in C2 adhesive. Screw the board every 8" into battens at 16" centres.

## Wastewater: biodigester, test chamber and reed bed

Your biodigester is 500 L (4 × 4 × 1.15 ft) and rated for five people. One person flushing a normal WC sends it roughly 20–30 L a day (my estimate), so toilet water stays in it for two to three weeks, far longer than it needs. Keep it for the toilet alone, check its outflow at a test chamber, settle the greywater separately, and polish both in a reed bed before the water soaks away.

&#91;image: Wastewater system on two terraces: WC and biodigester on the upper terrace; test chamber, junction, reed bed and soak trench on the lower one\]

### Setting the biodigester in its pit

From your photos the pit is dug and the ground looks firm. Before the tank goes in:

- **Bed it level:** pick out the sharp slate pieces, lay 2–3" of sand or a thin concrete pad, and check level both ways. The inlet should sit 8" below the upper terrace and the outlet about 12" above the lower one.
- **Fill as you backfill:** connect the pipes, fill the tank with water, then backfill evenly all round in 6" layers of soil or sand without sharp stones, tamping lightly. A full tank keeps its shape and can't float up in a wet monsoon.
- **Mind the dome's footing:** the pit sits right against the dome's base. Don't dig under any post or plinth, and if the pit edge is within a foot of one, build a stone or brick lining on that side.
- **Keep the lid reachable:** finish the lid at or just above ground, slope the soil away from it, and keep roof drip and terrace runoff off it. You'll open it to add bacteria.

### The test point

Make it a small chamber, not a tap. The outlet pipe is a gravity line, not under pressure, so a tap would only drain whatever sits at a low point, usually stale water and sludge rather than fresh outflow.

- **Where:** between the biodigester outlet and the point where greywater joins, so you test the biodigester alone.
- **How:** a 12 × 12" brick chamber standing on the lower terrace, its lid about 18" up, so you open it at knee height. The inlet pipe ends turned down, 6" above the outlet, so the flow falls in a stream you can catch in a jar. Bench the floor to the outlet so no water stands in it.
- **Test:** flush twice, lift the lid and catch the stream in a clear glass jar. Makers describe good outflow as odourless and colourless ([Organica Biotech](https://organicabiotech.com/how-does-a-biodigester-septic-tank-work-all-you-need-to-know/)); in practice expect clear to pale tea with a faint earthy smell, and little settling out in 30 minutes. Grey or black, murky, with floating bits, a rotten-egg smell or a layer of sludge means the bacteria are struggling.
- **Routine:** test monthly at the same time and photograph the jar against white. Outflow is slower and a little murkier in the coldest weeks, so retest after a mild spell before recharging. If it stays murky, recharge as the maker says, and check that no one has used phenyl, acid or bleach cleaners.

### Greywater: around the biodigester, not through it

Send shower, basin, kitchen and laundry water around the biodigester, not through it:

- Soap, detergent and kitchen grease slow the biodigester's bacteria.
- Soapy water is naturally cloudy, so mixing it in would make the jar test meaningless.
- Many biodigester makers sell their tanks for toilet waste only, so check that greywater wouldn't void your warranty.

Instead, give greywater its own pre-treatment and join it to the treated toilet water after the test chamber:

- **Grease trap** on the kitchen line; scoop it out monthly.
- **Settling tank**, two chambers, about 200–300 L, to catch hair, lint and food bits; desludge it yearly. Without these, the reed bed's gravel clogs.
- **Soaps:** use mild, biodegradable soaps and detergents.

### Size: one bed, 9 × 6 ft, 2 ft of gravel

The depth is right. A horizontal-flow reed bed is usually 0.5–1 m of gravel with the water kept 5–15 cm below the surface, and the usual area is 5–10 m² per person ([Akvopedia](https://akvopedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_Subsurface_Flow_Constructed_Wetland)).

- **Toilet outflow only:** a 4 × 7 ft bed (about 2.6 m²) would be enough for one person; the oversized biodigester does most of the work first.
- **Your 9 × 6 ft bed:** 54 sq ft, about 5 m², takes the toilet outflow and the greywater too (roughly 70–100 L a day for one person, my estimate). That is the low end of the usual range, reasonable after a generous biodigester and a settling tank, and guests for a few days are fine. One bed or two cells makes no difference to the treatment; only the total area matters.
- **Rain:** Naddi gets around 3,000 mm a year. A 100 mm monsoon downpour puts about 500 L on the bed, four or five days' worth of household water, so size the outlet and soak trench for it. The bed's walls, 9" above the lower terrace, keep runoff out.
- **Winter:** flow under the gravel keeps working, more slowly. The reeds die back; leave the stalks until spring.

**Levels on the lower terrace.** Your biodigester outlet is about 12" above the lower terrace, so the test chamber, the junction and the reed bed all go there. The test chamber stands partly above ground with a 6" drop for the jar. The bed's gravel surface sits 3" above ground with walls 9" up, so you dig only about 2 ft. The short pipes between the chambers run at ground level: cover them with soil or slate against sun and frost.

### What makes the walls

The bed must be watertight; Akvopedia calls for an impermeable liner. The drawings use stone walls with plaster and a liner inside:

- **Stone walls (as drawn):** 9" stone in 1:4 cement on a 3" concrete base, 1" lower at the outlet end, up to 9" above the lower terrace. Plaster inside with waterproofing compound, then a 0.5 mm HDPE liner between two layers of geotextile. Pond-test for 48 hours before any gravel goes in.
- **Lined pit (cheaper):** dig with sloped sides, lay a sand cushion, the liner and geotextile, and hold the liner edge under a stone kerb. It works, but the walls have to stand 9" above ground anyway, so stone walls all round are simpler.

Either way, keep the top 4–6" above the ground around it.

Inside the walls:

- **Gravel:** 1½–3" (40–80 mm) stone in a 1'-6" zone at each end; washed ½–¾" (10–20 mm) gravel in between with 3" of pea gravel on top, all without fines.
- **Pipes:** a 4" perforated spreader across the inlet end, 2" below the surface; a 4" perforated collector on the floor at the outlet end; a capped cleanout riser on each.
- **Water level:** 4" below the surface, set by a swivel elbow in the outlet chamber. Turn it down to drain the bed.
- **Plants:** common reed (Phragmites), 16–20 cuttings 18" apart, planted in spring. Ask a local nursery for reeds that survive frost.

### Where the treated water goes

A soak trench on the same terrace or the one below: a perforated pipe in gravel, well away from any spring, bowli or well. It can also water trees and ornamental plants below ground, but not vegetables eaten raw.

### Pipe sizes

Use PVC SWR pipe, Type B, with a wall of about 3.2 mm; it is the grade made for soil and waste water. Type A is thinner and meant only for rainwater and vents ([Finolex](https://www.finolexpipes.com/swr-pipes-fittings/swr-selfit-pvc-pipes-2)).

- **WC to biodigester:** 110 mm (4"), falling 1:40.
- **Biodigester to test chamber, junction and reed bed:** 110 mm (4"), to match the tank's outlet. The flow is small, but a 4" line is easy to rod and won't block.
- **Spreader and collector in the bed:** 110 mm perforated.
- **Greywater:** 40–50 mm (1½–2") from the basin and shower, 50 mm (2") from the kitchen sink and washing machine, joining a 75 mm (3") main to the settling tank and on to the junction. Fall 1:40.
- **Outlet chamber to soak trench:** 110 mm, then 110 mm perforated in the trench.
- **Above ground:** paint or cover any pipe left in the sun; PVC turns brittle in sunlight.

**Before digging**, check two things. With a clear water-level tube, confirm the biodigester outlet really sits about 12" above the lower terrace, since every level on the reed bed sheets follows from it. And ask the maker how often, and with what, to recharge the bacteria.

## Verify on site before building

Five measurements decide whether the layout holds as drawn; the back width matters most.

- [ ] Back wall base width, and depth from the front base line to the back base line (assumed 7'-0" and 7'-0").
- [ ] Lean of the front and back walls, with a plumb line from the apex (assumed 15°).
- [ ] Depth from finished floor level to firm ground under the extension, which sets the fill and plinth height.
- [ ] Route and fall to the septic tank or sewer for a 110 mm soil pipe, and the water pressure at the shower (a rain head needs good pressure; low pressure means a booster pump).
- [ ] Purlin depth behind the exterior panels, which sets the insulation thickness, and whether the exterior side panels are fully watertight: on this building the "walls" are really roofs.
